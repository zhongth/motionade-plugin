# Motion composition

Use for an original film, substantial redesign, or a sequence whose compositions feel like presentation pages. Reconstruction uses the reference's existing design decisions unless adaptation is requested. A timing-only edit keeps accepted composition intact.

## Responsibility

Turn the message into a visual premise and a sequence of connected viewing experiences. Decide what carries meaning, how framing and hierarchy develop, and what the viewer follows next. [Choreography](choreography.md) resolves participants and relationships; [temporal design](temporal-design.md) resolves their timing and behavior. Iterate between them during design. Detailed asset production follows a convincing rough. Establish shared typography, palette, recurring forms, movement character, and any material/depth treatment; let composition develop within that visual language.

## Find the visual premise

Express what the viewer understands through a visible change. “An illustration beside a claim” describes content placement; the premise also needs a reason for movement, reveal, contrast, repetition, or stillness. A product demonstration can let an action prove its benefit. Typography can become the subject: its scale, grouping, replacement, or relationship to a shape can carry the idea. Preserve enough literal product information to avoid a stylish but unintelligible sequence.

When the idea is generic, sketch a genuinely different visual treatment before committing to detailed assets. Compare how each communicates the message at the intended speed. Choose using the brief; multiple polished pitches or a fixed number of alternatives are unnecessary.

Do not assume a reference's visual grammar has transferred merely because its construction technique works. Study how it chooses subjects, leaves information out, varies scale, and connects ideas. Adapt those decisions to the new meaning. A source's exact motif, copy, and sequence remain local choices.

## Compose moments and the change between them

For each important phrase, resolve the focal subject and meaningful states as actual inspectable compositions. Use essential copy, representative product states, type size and wrapping, crop, contrast, spacing, and negative space at the intended aspect ratio. These frames need enough visual resolution to judge the idea before detailed animation; neutral boxes or a palette paragraph leave hierarchy and framing undecided. Secondary drawing details can wait. A compact board, native stills, or another inspectable format can serve this purpose.

Beside the important frames, briefly identify what becomes understandable, what attracts attention first, and what connects to the preceding and following moment. Distinguish information the viewer must read from shapes or states they only need to recognize and context that can remain subordinate. This determines how much detail earns emphasis. Use the fewest frames that resolve the design questions; a short passage may need only a meaningful before/after pair. The board is revisable design evidence, not another authoritative timeline.

Useful decisions include:

- **Scale and framing:** when the subject is a detail, an object in space, a full-frame field, or a deliberately cropped form. Close views can expose an action; wider views can reveal its consequence.
- **Typography as action:** what the main word does that helps the idea register. An incidental entrance on a fixed headline has a different role from a word that changes the composition.
- **Transformation and reveal:** what is retained, replaced, multiplied, or uncovered, and how the viewer recognizes the relationship. A cut can carry the connection through matched framing, direction, shape, or a deliberate contrast.
- **Sequence development:** how the next phrase extends, answers, or changes the previous one. Reuse a composition when the repeated comparison has a purpose. Reusing identical title and illustration slots across unrelated sections needs reconsideration.
- **Understanding and progression:** what the viewer comes to understand or expect as the composition changes, how the design supports it, and when the next event can build on it. Count useful exposure throughout the phrase and give any additional rest a specific communication or expressive purpose.

A stable product surface with changing internal content can be the right design for a demonstration. Later phrases can change framing or visual subject when their meaning changes. Stability is a local choice, not a whole-film layout template.

## Let the purpose determine exposure

A design frame can represent a passing accent, a reveal, an interaction, a readable result, or another useful state within continuous motion. Its presence on the board does not imply a hold. Give the moment provisional time according to what must register and what its neighbors depend on. A brief orientation change may need less than a second; a product interaction may develop over several seconds. Neither is a default duration. Several designed states may belong to one gesture, and one composition may develop through multiple actions.

Try those relationships in a timed native rough, including the central action and neighboring handoffs. Count useful exposure during movement, using [Temporal design](temporal-design.md#design-how-understanding-develops). If a moment needs more time, inspect its information hierarchy and competing activity along with its timing. Revise the frames and rough together: timing can expose a weak composition, and a clearer composition can change the time needed. Keep provisional durations out of reusable recipes.

## Copy economy for fast promos

Start with the main phrase and the visible action. Do not automatically add an eyebrow, descriptive subtitle, context badge, footer slogan, and repeated brand label. Remove optional copy that only repeats what the main phrase or action already communicates. If a line carries necessary information, give it a deliberate reading opportunity or turn it into a main beat.

Keep requested wording, accessibility captions, necessary instructions, disclosures, and useful product-state labels. A dense explainer or deliberate editorial composition may need supporting text. Judge its role and exposure rather than applying a blanket ban or arbitrary word-count limit.

Avoid compensating for weak composition with ornamental assets, extra labels, constant background motion, or faster entrances. First resolve what changes meaningfully on screen.

## Review before full production

Inspect the actual frames before building detailed motion, at a size representative of delivery. Resolve unclear focus, crop, typography, and neighboring relationships while they are cheap to change. Then inspect the rough of the central sequence with its handoffs. Resolve these questions before costly detailing:

1. Can the viewer understand the promise through the dominant subject and its behavior? Does the product still have a recognizable identity?
2. Does the sequence develop visually, or could successive moments be made by swapping a title, subtitle, and illustration into the same slots? Repetition can pass when its purpose is explicit and fits the brief.
3. Is optional explanatory copy carrying work that the composition should perform? Can it be removed without losing the message?
4. Do the focal handoff, scale changes, and information progression produce the requested energy? A coherent design may use abrupt cuts, smooth travel, or purposeful pauses.
5. Does the rough work between the attractive poses? Check actual visibility, travel, reading, and incoming activity. A style-frame board cannot establish temporal success.

Repair the earliest unresolved decision. A weak idea calls for concept work; a repetitive sequence calls for recomposition or editing; a poor join calls for choreography/timing; a drawing detail calls for polish. Report which level is being repaired. “Clean and readable” is a limited observation, not creative acceptance.

One worked decision from a scheduling promo: the board initially placed “Confirmed” separately from the button that triggered it. Reviewing the neighboring frames suggested retaining the button's dark plate and expanding it into the result bar, with the selected time and people still present. That changed the construction before animation began. The transferable question is what existing visual relationship can make a state change understandable. Expanding a button is one answer for that composition; other content may call for a cut, replacement, persistence, or a different relationship.

## Map design to editable Scenes

Name the intended sequences and their native Scene/group mapping before detailed authoring. A board frame marks a design state and does not automatically create a native Scene. Prefer native Scenes for chapters that benefit from separate selection, duration editing, rearrangement, or replacement. Several brief beats may belong to one sequence; a continuous shot may stay in one Scene. Explain a whole-film single-Scene choice when the brief contains distinct sequences. Shared rig convenience alone is insufficient justification.

Preserve visual identity across boundaries with a supported transition, matched outgoing/incoming instances, or a continuous group where appropriate. Verify the actual cut/reveal, scale, motion phase, and music clock. [Native authoring](authoring.md#scene-structure-and-cross-scene-continuity) owns the current implementation limits. Scene count and visible momentum are separate quality questions.
